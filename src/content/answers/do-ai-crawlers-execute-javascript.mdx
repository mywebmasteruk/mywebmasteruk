## Why this matters more than it sounds

A large category of SEO automation tools works by injecting changes into the page with
JavaScript: you paste a snippet into your `<head>`, and the tool rewrites titles, adds schema
markup and injects content in the browser after the page loads.

For traditional search this mostly works, because Googlebot renders JavaScript. For answer
engines it does not work at all. If the crawler never runs the script, the "optimised" version
of the page never exists as far as it is concerned. It sees the original HTML.

The uncomfortable implication: tools sold on the promise of getting you cited in ChatGPT or
Perplexity, which deliver that promise through client-side injection, are shipping changes to
the one audience that cannot see them.

## The server-side workaround has its own problem

The enterprise alternative is to rewrite HTML at the edge or the server, and serve the improved
version to crawlers while human visitors get the original. That is visible to AI crawlers, but
it means bots and people receive different content — which is dynamic rendering. Google
[stopped recommending it](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/dynamic-rendering)
and describes it as a workaround rather than a solution, and serving materially different
content to crawlers is adjacent to cloaking.

## What this means in practice

If you want to be readable by answer engines, the reliable approach is for the improvement to
exist in the HTML the server sends, to everyone, identically. That is an argument for changing
the source of the site rather than layering a rewrite on top of it — which is why
[Autopilot commits to your repository](/how-it-works/) instead of injecting into your pages.

There is a second, quieter benefit. A change committed to your repository is yours. A change
injected by a subscription script disappears the day the subscription stops.
